# Vocal Acoustics & Biomechanics of Human Speech Production

---

## 1. Executive Overview

Human speech production is a non-linear, aero-acoustic, biomechanical process that converts aerodynamic energy from the respiratory system into acoustic sound waves radiating from the mouth and nostrils. For autonomous robotics, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and embedded voice-operated systems, understanding the biomechanics of speech is essential for designing robust acoustic frontends, feature extractors, and keyword spotting (KWS) classifiers.

This document formulates the physical, anatomical, and mathematical foundations of human speech generation, from subglottal pressure dynamics to vocal tract acoustic resonance and radiation impedance.

---

## 2. The Source-Filter Model of Speech (Fant Formulation)

The classical acoustic theory of speech production, pioneered by Gunnar Fant (1960), models speech as the linear convolution of three distinct physical stages:

$$s(t) = g(t) * v(t) * r(t)$$

In the frequency domain via Laplace/Fourier transform:

$$S(f) = G(f) \cdot V(f) \cdot R(f)$$

Where:
- $G(f)$ is the **Glottal Source Spectrum**: The acoustic volume velocity flow generated by the vibrating vocal folds at the glottis.
- $V(f)$ is the **Vocal Tract Transfer Function**: The acoustic filter response determined by the geometric shape, cross-sectional area function, and boundary conditions of the pharyngeal, oral, and nasal cavities.
- $R(f)$ is the **Lip Radiation Impedance**: The high-pass filtering effect occurring as acoustic waves transition from the enclosed vocal tract tube to the open acoustic free field.

```mermaid
    Lungs["Lungs & Trachea<br/>(Subglottal Pressure P_sub)"] --> Glottis["Vocal Folds / Glottis<br/>Source G(f)"]
    Glottis --> Tract["Vocal Tract Cavity<br/>Filter V(f) (Formants F1-F4)"]
    Tract --> Lips["Lip Radiation Impedance<br/>R(f) (+6 dB/octave)"]
    Lips --> Radiated["Radiated Speech Wave<br/>S(f)"]
```

## 3. Biomechanics of Glottal Excitation

### 3.1 Subglottal Pressure & Aerodynamic Driving Forces
Speech begins with pulmonary compression. The lungs contract via intercostal and abdominal muscle activation, generating an elevated subglottal pressure $P_{\text{sub}}$ typically between $400\text{ Pa}$ and $2000\text{ Pa}$ ($4\text{ to }20\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$):
- Conversational speech: $P_{\text{sub}} \approx 600\text{ - }800\text{ Pa}$.
- Shouting / Lombard speech in high noise: $P_{\text{sub}} \ge 1500\text{ - }3000\text{ Pa}$.

### 3.2 The Myoelastic-Aerodynamic Theory & Bernoulli Principle
Vocal fold vibration is self-sustained through the interaction of tissue elasticity (myoelastic properties) and aerodynamic forces:
1. **Adduction**: Vocal folds are held in close approximation by the intrinsic laryngeal muscles (lateral cricoarytenoid, interarytenoid, and thyroarytenoid muscles).
2. **Pressure Accumulation**: Subglottal pressure $P_{\text{sub}}$ forces the inferior margins of the vocal folds apart.
3. **Volume Velocity Flow**: Air rushes through the narrow glottal constriction with velocity $u_g$. According to Bernoulli's principle, the static intraglottal pressure $P_g$ drops:
   $$P_g = P_{\text{sub}} - \frac{1}{2} \rho u_g^2$$
4. **Tissue Re-Closure**: The negative Bernoulli pressure, combined with the lateral elastic restoring forces of the vocal fold tissues and mucosal wave mechanics, rapidly snaps the folds back together.

### 3.3 Two-Mass Model of Vocal Fold Dynamics (Ishizaka-Flanagan)
To capture vertical phase differences (the mucosal wave propagating from the lower to the upper lip of the fold), the tissue is mathematically discretized into coupled mechanical oscillators:

$$m_1 \ddot{x}_1 + r_1 \dot{x}_1 + k_1 x_1 + k_c (x_1 - x_2) = F_1(P_g)$$
$$m_2 \ddot{x}_2 + r_2 \dot{x}_2 + k_2 x_2 + k_c (x_2 - x_1) = F_2(P_g)$$

Where:
- $m_1, m_2$ are the lower and upper vocal fold masses per unit length.
- $k_1, k_2$ are non-linear tissue stiffness parameters.
- $k_c$ is the vertical coupling spring constant.
- $F_1, F_2$ are the integrated aerodynamic pressures acting across the fold surfaces.

This phase lag between $x_1$ and $x_2$ sustains continuous self-oscillation without external driving energy.

### 3.4 Glottal Volume Velocity Waveform & Spectral Tilt
The resulting glottal volume velocity pulse $u_g(t)$ is asymmetric:
- **Opening Phase**: Slow, gradual rise as folds are pushed apart.
- **Closing Phase**: Rapid, steep descent caused by the combined elastic snap and Bernoulli suction.
- **Closed Phase**: Zero or minimal volume flow.

The derivative of the glottal flow, $\dot{u}_g(t)$, exhibits an abrupt negative peak at the point of complete closure ($t_e$, the moment of glottal closing). This instant injects the primary acoustic energy into the vocal tract.

#### Glottal Spectral Tilt:
Due to this asymmetric triangular volume flow, the glottal excitation spectrum $G(f)$ rolls off asymptotically at:
$$-12\text{ dB/octave} \quad (\sim 1/f^2)$$

For breathy voices (incomplete glottal closure), the spectral tilt steepens to $-18\text{ dB/octave}$ with excessive high-frequency aspiration noise. For pressed or tense voices (loud commands, stress), closure is abrupt, flattening the spectral tilt to $-6\text{ dB/octave}$, injecting substantial high-frequency harmonic energy into the system.

## 4. Fundamental Frequency ($F_0$) Dynamics

The fundamental frequency $F_0$ (pitch) corresponds to the inverse of the glottal cycle period $T_0$:

$$F_0 = \frac{1}{T_0}$$

$F_0$ is mechanically governed by the effective vibrating length $L$, tissue density $\rho_t$, and longitudinal tension $\sigma$ of the vocal fold cover:

$$F_0 \approx \frac{1}{2L} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho_t}}$$

Tension $\sigma$ is modulated dynamically by the **cricothyroid (CT) muscle**, which tilts the thyroid cartilage forward, elongating and thinning the vocal folds.

### 4.1 Population Distributions of $F_0$
- **Adult Males**: Mean $F_0 \approx 110\text{ - }130\text{ Hz}$ (typical operational range: $85\text{ - }180\text{ Hz}$).
- **Adult Females**: Mean $F_0 \approx 200\text{ - }220\text{ Hz}$ (typical operational range: $165\text{ - }260\text{ Hz}$).
- **Children (under 10 years)**: Mean $F_0 \approx 280\text{ - }350\text{ Hz}$ (operational range: $250\text{ - }500+\text{ Hz}$).

### 4.2 Impact on Acoustic Feature Extraction
The separation between harmonics in the frequency spectrum equals $F_0$. For a male speaker with $F_0 = 100\text{ Hz}$, harmonic peaks occur every $100\text{ Hz}$, allowing fine spectral sampling of the vocal tract transfer function $V(f)$. For a female or child speaker with $F_0 = 350\text{ Hz}$, harmonic samples occur only every $350\text{ Hz}$, undersampling the vocal tract formants and introducing pitch-dependent ripples into short-time Fourier transforms (STFT) and Mel-filterbank energies.

## 5. Acoustic Filter of the Vocal Tract & Formant Physics

### 5.1 Acoustic Wave Equation in a Lossless Tube
The vocal tract can be idealized as an acoustic waveguide with variable cross-sectional area $A(x, t)$ along the centerline coordinate $x \in [0, L_{\text{tract}}]$, where $x = 0$ is the glottis and $x = L_{\text{tract}}$ is the lips.

Assuming planar acoustic wave propagation (valid for frequencies below $f \approx \frac{c}{2d_{\text{max}}} \approx 4000\text{ - }5000\text{ Hz}$ where cross-sectional dimensions are smaller than half a wavelength):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left[ A(x) \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} \right] = \frac{A(x)}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial t^2}$$

Where:
- $p(x, t)$ is the acoustic perturbation pressure.
- $c \approx 343\text{ m/s}$ is the speed of sound in warm moist air ($37^\circ\text{C}$).

### 5.2 Uniform Quarter-Wave Resonator (Neutral Vowel /ə/)
For a uniform cylindrical tube of length $L_{\text{tract}} \approx 17.5\text{ cm}$ with closed glottis boundary condition ($u(0) = 0$, velocity node / pressure antinode) and open lip boundary condition ($p(L) = 0$, pressure node / velocity antinode):

$$\lambda_n = \frac{4 L_{\text{tract}}}{2n - 1}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

The formant resonance frequencies are:

$$F_n = \frac{(2n - 1) c}{4 L_{\text{tract}}}$$

For an adult male with $L_{\text{tract}} = 17.5\text{ cm}$:
- $F_1 = \frac{1 \cdot 34300\text{ cm/s}}{4 \cdot 17.5\text{ cm}} = 490\text{ Hz} \approx 500\text{ Hz}$
- $F_2 = \frac{3 \cdot 34300}{70} = 1470\text{ Hz} \approx 1500\text{ Hz}$
- $F_3 = \frac{5 \cdot 34300}{70} = 2450\text{ Hz} \approx 2500\text{ Hz}$
- $F_4 = \frac{7 \cdot 34300}{70} = 3430\text{ Hz} \approx 3500\text{ Hz}$

### 5.3 Perturbation Theory (Stevens & House / Chiba & Kajiyama)
Local constrictions in the vocal tract shift formant frequencies according to the distribution of acoustic kinetic and potential energy:

$$\frac{\Delta F_n}{F_n} = \frac{\Delta V}{V} \left( \frac{E_{k,n} - E_{p,n}}{E_{\text{total},n}} \right)$$

- **Constriction at a Velocity Antinode (Pressure Node)**: Kinetic energy dominates. Constricting the tube lowers the formant frequency ($\Delta F_n < 0$).
- **Constriction at a Pressure Antinode (Velocity Node)**: Potential energy dominates. Constricting the tube raises the formant frequency ($\Delta F_n > 0$).

#### Practical Consequences:
1. **Lip Rounding / Protrusion**: Always occurs at a velocity antinode (open end of the tube). Lip rounding lengthens $L_{\text{tract}}$ and lowers **all** formant frequencies ($F_1, F_2, F_3$).
2. **Tongue Body Height (Vocalic Openness)**: Lowering the jaw and tongue enlarges the pharyngeal cavity and constricts the region near the glottis (pressure antinode for $F_1$), **raising $F_1$** ($/i/ \to /a/$, $F_1: 270\text{ Hz} \to 750\text{ Hz}$).
3. **Tongue Fronting / Backing**: Advancing the tongue blade forward creates a constriction near the velocity node for $F_2$ while expanding the pharynx, **raising $F_2$** ($/u/ \to /i/$, $F_2: 850\text{ Hz} \to 2300\text{ Hz}$).

## 6. Lip Radiation Impedance & Net Speech Spectrum

### 6.1 Radiation from a Sphere / Piston in an Infinite Baffle
At the lips, the propagating planar wave exits an aperture of radius $a \approx 1\text{ - }2\text{ cm}$. The radiation impedance $Z_{\text{rad}}(\omega)$ is modeled as a circular piston in a spherical baffle:

$$Z_{\text{rad}}(\omega) \approx \frac{\rho \omega^2}{4\pi c} + j \frac{\rho \omega}{\pi a}$$

For frequencies below $4000\text{ Hz}$ where $\frac{\omega a}{c} \ll 1$, the acoustic pressure radiated into the far-field is proportional to the **time derivative of the lip volume velocity**:

$$p_{\text{far}}(t) \propto \frac{\partial u_{\text{lip}}(t)}{\partial t}$$

### 6.2 Radiation Spectral Slope (+6 dB/Octave)
In the frequency domain, differentiation corresponds to multiplication by $j\omega$:

$$R(f) \propto f \implies +6\text{ dB/octave}$$

### 6.3 Net Spectral Balance of Radiated Speech
Combining all three components of Fant's source-filter model:
1. **Glottal Source $G(f)$**: $-12\text{ dB/octave}$ roll-off.
2. **Vocal Tract Filter $V(f)$**: Average flat response ($0\text{ dB/octave}$) modulated by resonant formant peaks ($+15\text{ to }+25\text{ dB}$ resonance amplification with typical bandwidths $B_1 \approx 50\text{ Hz}, B_2 \approx 70\text{ Hz}, B_3 \approx 100\text{ Hz}$).
3. **Lip Radiation $R(f)$**: $+6\text{ dB/octave}$ high-pass tilt.

$$\text{Net Radiated Spectrum Slope} = -12\text{ dB/octave} + 0\text{ dB/octave} + 6\text{ dB/octave} = -6\text{ dB/octave}$$

Radiated human speech naturally exhibits an overall **$-6\text{ dB/octave}$ spectral tilt**. This fundamental physical characteristic is why high-performance speech engines (including Sonon) apply a **pre-emphasis filter**:

$$y[n] = x[n] - \alpha x[n-1], \quad \alpha \in [0.95, 0.98]$$

Which implements a $+6\text{ dB/octave}$ high-frequency shelf, perfectly flattening the radiated speech spectrum to ensure equal numerical weighting across low ($F_1$) and high ($F_2, F_3$) formant frequencies during STFT and Mel filterbank extraction.

## 7. Acoustic Phonetics & Phoneme Classification

Speech sounds are partitioned into distinct acoustic categories based on excitation mechanisms and tract configurations:

| Category | Excitation Mechanism | Acoustic Signature | Examples in Wake Words |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Vowels** | Periodic glottal pulses | Clear harmonic structure, stable formant peaks ($F_1, F_2, F_3$), high energy | /o/ in "hold", /æ/ in "land" |
| **Diphthongs** | Glottal pulses + continuous articulatory movement | Dynamic trajectories in $F_1$ and $F_2$ over time | /eɪ/ in "take off" |
| **Plosives / Stops** | Complete tract closure $\to$ pressure build $\to$ transient release burst | Silence interval (closure), high-energy transient spike ($< 20\text{ ms}$), Voice Onset Time (VOT), rapid formant transitions | /t/, /k/ in "take off" |
| **Fricatives** | Turbulent jet airflow through narrow tract constriction | Aperiodic broad-band noise, high spectral center of gravity, lack of pitch harmonics | /s/ in "stop", /f/ in "take off" |
| **Affricates** | Stop closure followed immediately by fricative release | Brief silence followed by sudden rise in turbulent noise | /tʃ/ in "check" |
| **Nasals** | Velum lowered, oral tract closed, sound radiated through nasal cavity | Low nasal murmur ($F_0 \approx 250\text{ Hz}$), high damping (large cavity bandwidth), anti-formants (zeros in spectrum) | /n/ in "land", /m/ in "mission" |
| **Approximants / Liquids** | Partial open tract without turbulence | Vowel-like formant structure, lower amplitude, low $F_3$ for American English /r/ | /l/ in "land", /r/ in "return" |

## 8. Summary of Biomechanical Implications for Embedded KWS

1. **Pre-Emphasis Necessity**: Without a $+6\text{ dB/octave}$ pre-emphasis filter ($\alpha \approx 0.97$), acoustic feature extractors will heavily over-weight $F_1$ (< 800 Hz) while losing critical consonant distinction features ($F_2, F_3$, fricatives) in the numerical noise floor.
2. **Formant Invariance vs. Pitch Independence**: A robust wake-word classifier must recognize words regardless of the operator's $F_0$ (pitch) by focusing strictly on the envelope $V(f)$ rather than individual glottal harmonic spikes.
3. **Consonant Energy Ratios**: High-frequency consonants (/s/, /t/, /k/, /f/) carry up to 80% of linguistic information in wake words like "take off" and "abort", but possess less than 10% of total acoustic energy compared to vowels. Feature extractors must maintain high dynamic range and proper normalization to prevent consonants from being masked by ambient noise.
